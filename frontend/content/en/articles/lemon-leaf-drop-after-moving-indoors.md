The lemon tree is indoors at last. Nights on the balcony have turned cold, and there is a lovely spot waiting by the window. Then leaves start appearing below the pot. One, then another. It is tempting to water, feed it or move it somewhere warmer. First, take a moment to see how much changed with that move.

**A lemon can shed leaves as it adjusts to different light, temperatures and humidity indoors.** Two observations help: how much light reaches the tree away from radiator heat and cold draughts, and how moist the compost is deeper in the pot. Those observations will help you decide what to change.

## The same daylight, a different amount of light

On the balcony, light reached the canopy from several directions. Indoors, there may be glass, curtains, a deep window recess and some distance between the tree and the sky. A room that feels bright to you can still offer the plant much less light.

Daylight can reveal a curtain shading part of the canopy while the lower branches sit in shadow. Sometimes drawing the curtain back or moving the pot closer helps, with the leaves kept clear of cold glass. Choose the brightest suitable window; in a dim winter position, a grow light can help. This [windowsill lighting guide](/en/articles/winter-grow-lights-for-houseplants/) explains how to position one.

If the move is still ahead, ease the transition by gradually getting the tree used to a less bright position before bringing it inside. Once indoors, choose a suitable place and observe it there rather than moving it to a new corner every evening.

## What the pot can tell you

A fallen leaf can make you reach straight for the watering can, even when water is still sitting inside the decorative outer pot. The opposite is possible too: the compost looks dark on top, but the root ball has dried out. Either overly wet or overly dry conditions can lead to leaf loss.

Gently check the compost about 4–5 cm (roughly 2 inches) below the surface, near the edge where you can reach it. Lift the pot as well. Over time, you will learn how much lighter it feels as the compost dries.

| What you find | What to do now |
| --- | --- |
| Wet compost deeper down, with water in the saucer or outer pot | Empty the water, check that the drainage holes are clear and allow the compost to dry further before watering |
| Compost at that depth is becoming dry and the pot feels lighter | Water thoroughly until it drains through the holes, then remove the excess |
| A dry surface with moist compost beneath | Check again later; the dry surface alone does not describe the whole root ball |

The summer watering interval often needs to change indoors. Compost may stay moist longer in cooler conditions and weaker light; heating can create a different situation again. Set a reminder to check the pot, then water according to what you find.

## Room temperature is not the whole story

Beside a radiator the air can be hot and dry, while an open window sends a cold draught across the tree. The rest of the room may feel perfectly comfortable. A thermometer beside the plant will show the conditions during the day and evening, especially when airing the room.

A position with good light and no hot air or cold draughts blowing across the canopy and pot suits this move. A room humidifier can help with dry air. Extra water around the roots cannot take the place of suitable air humidity.

A cool winter position still needs to suit your particular lemon. Before leaving it on an enclosed balcony, check the cultivar's requirements and the overnight temperature beside the pot. A general preference for cooler conditions does not establish a safe minimum for every citrus tree.

## Look under the leaves

As you check the position, examine the leaf undersides, stalks and young shoots. Spider mites and scale insects can affect citrus. If you find pests or webbing, move the lemon away from other plants while you identify the problem. More watering will not deal with that cause.

A single yellow leaf tells you little about the tree's nutrient supply. The fuller picture is more useful: which leaves are falling, how wet the root ball is, when the tree came inside and what temperatures it has experienced.

## Feeding and repotting have their place

Choose fertilizer to match the plant's growth, growing medium and product directions. There is no special rescue dose for leaf drop after a move. While the tree is adjusting and growth has slowed, work on light and watering first; revisit feeding with a clear purpose.

Routine repotting is usually easier at the start of growth, often in spring. If the compost stays wet for a long time, smells unpleasant and the tree continues to decline, the roots and drainage need closer assessment. Moving indoors alone is no reason to dismantle a healthy root ball.

For the next few days, a short record can help: moving date, position, moisture deeper in the pot, watering and a photo of the canopy, with a note on whether leaf loss continues. You will have something more useful than a worried impression that things are getting worse: observations that help show what the tree still needs.

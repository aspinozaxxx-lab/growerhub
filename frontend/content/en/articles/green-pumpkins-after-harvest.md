Autumn has a way of hurrying things along. Yesterday the pumpkins were tucked beneath their leaves. Today frost is in the forecast, and the whole harvest is indoors. Heavy fruits crowd the kitchen table, bringing a little of the garden with them. It is time to put the kettle on. Except one pumpkin is still green. Does it need warmth, sunshine, or simply a little more time?

The answer starts back in the garden. A green pumpkin may already be mature: some varieties are meant to stay that colour. But a fruit picked very young will not become the equal of one that matured on the plant. Skin can change colour after harvest without promising sweet flesh or a long storage life.

## Some pumpkins are meant to be green

There is no single colour of ripeness. Green-skinned varieties can keep their colour when mature. In kabocha, for example, bright green grows duller and the stem becomes corky. An orange pumpkin follows a different path, developing rich, even colour and a hard rind.

This is when the forgotten seed packet comes in handy. The variety name helps you recognise what a mature fruit should look like. Without it, a photograph of a similar pumpkin online is unlikely to settle the question. A fruit of uncertain maturity is better kept separately and watched, rather than counted on to last all winter.

## Choosing the winter keepers

Mature edible pumpkins with intact, hard rinds and their stems still attached are the best candidates for long storage. They are the fruits you hope to return to when the garden season feels a long way behind you.

A stem looks like a convenient handle, but it can snap under a pumpkin's weight. Supporting the fruit from underneath, as you would a heavy bowl, is kinder to it. Fewer knocks and cuts on the journey from the garden mean a better start to storage.

Some fruits should leave the stock straight away. Soft patches, mould or spreading decay are reasons to remove a pumpkin. A warm kitchen will not put those right.

Cold needs a little more thought. Picking ahead of a forecast frost is quite different from bringing in fruit that actually froze. The forecast alone does not mean the harvest was damaged. But once tissue has frozen, warmth cannot restore it, and that pumpkin is unsuitable for long storage.

## What a little warmth can do

Pumpkins often spend time in warm conditions after harvest before moving into storage. This preparation, known as curing, helps harden the rind and heal small scratches, giving the harvest a better chance of keeping through the months ahead.

It is easy to mistake that for ripening. Curing mainly helps preserve a harvest that was already mature. It cannot give a very early-picked fruit everything it missed on the plant, or repair freezing damage.

The right approach depends on the type. Acorn squash should not be warm-cured at all: it reduces quality and storage life. A warm corner for every fruit in the batch is therefore not a reliable plan.

## Finding a good place for the harvest

Pumpkins look lovely in a kitchen. Beside a wooden board, a ceramic baking dish and a linen tea towel, they make an autumn still life all by themselves. For long storage, though, they usually do better somewhere dry and airy, with cool temperatures and no risk of freezing.

For ordinary pumpkins, a useful guide is **50–55 °F**, or about **10–13 °C**. Other types have their own preferences: guidance for many winter squash gives **12.5–15 °C (55–59 °F)**, while green-rinded varieties may need different conditions. Knowing the variety is more helpful here than a general instruction to put everything in a cellar.

A balcony or garage works only if temperatures beside the fruit stay suitable overnight as well. A thermometer on the shelf will soon tell you what the harvest is actually experiencing. An ordinary refrigerator is often too cold for a long stay: many winter squash suffer chilling injury after extended storage below 10 °C (50 °F), even without freezing.

Fruit arranged in a single layer, with gaps for air, is easy to see and reach. Softening or early decay is easier to notice that way, so a spoiling pumpkin can be removed before it becomes an unpleasant surprise.

And eventually there will be an evening for thick pumpkin soup or a pie. Rain at the window, the garden resting, and the scent of autumn back in the kitchen. Those are the evenings worth saving a harvest for.

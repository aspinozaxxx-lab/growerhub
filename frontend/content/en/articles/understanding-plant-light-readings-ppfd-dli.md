After your first reading, you want to get on with caring for the plant: leave the pot where it is, move it closer to the lamp or turn up the light. But the number on the screen describes the moment you took it. Two shelves can be equally bright at leaf level while one lamp runs only in the evening and the other starts in the morning. Over the whole day, their plants receive different amounts of light.

Three labels help make sense of this: lux, PPFD and DLI. Lux is useful for comparing positions under one source. PPFD describes the light arriving at the leaves now; DLI describes how much arrives over the day. Adding the lamp’s operating hours to your note makes a single reading much more useful.

## Check the units beside the number

A note saying “light: 200” leaves too much to guess. A reading of 200 lux and a PPFD of 200 µmol/m²/s are different quantities. Keeping the units, measuring position and source beside the number makes it much easier to compare later.

Lux is weighted to human vision. PPFD counts the photon flux within a defined range, per unit area each second. If a plant recommendation uses PPFD, a lux value cannot simply take its place. Conversion depends on the spectrum: a factor from the first calculator you find may describe a different lamp.

The [light measurement guide](/en/articles/measuring-light-for-plants-phone-lux-par/) helps you choose a method and take the first reading. Here we are looking at what to do with the result.

## One lamp, two different days

Imagine a shelf lit only by a lamp. At the selected point, PPFD stays around **200 µmol/m²/s** throughout its operation. Window light is excluded from this example.

Over 12 hours, the lamp delivers about **8.6 mol/m² per day**. Keep the same 200 but run it for four hours, and the contribution is about **2.9 mol/m²**. The brightness at the moment of measurement is unchanged, while the daily amount differs threefold.

That accumulated daily amount is called **DLI**. For constant light, the calculation is:

**DLI = PPFD × operating hours × 0.0036.**

For our example: **200 × 12 × 0.0036 = 8.64**. A rounded 8.6 is easier to keep in a note. Extra decimal places cannot make the result more precise than the original measurement.

This gives you a way to compare two schedules for the same lamp. Choosing the amount that suits your plant is the next step: a rooting cutting, a growing plant and one preparing to flower may need different conditions.

## What to compare with your DLI

Your note now says 8.6 mol/m² per day. To work out whether that is enough, find a reference for your plant species and check what it measures. Compare DLI with DLI, and a PPFD reading at the leaves with a PPFD range. Keep the units the same.

A cutting developing roots and an established flowering plant may need different treatment. A production recommendation for a greenhouse can also serve a different purpose from keeping a houseplant through winter. A useful reference explains the crop, stage and conditions it describes.

If a result falls only just outside a recommended range, consider the measurement's uncertainty first. A small spectral-conversion error or a different receiver position can change the conclusion. In a close case, repeating the reading and checking with a suitable meter is more useful than immediately buying another lamp.

## The middle of the shelf is only one place

It is tempting to apply a pleasing centre reading to the whole collection. Pots at the edges may receive less light, and a tall plant may shade its neighbours. A map of several points reveals the difference.

DLI also belongs to a particular point. Under a steady lamp, you can calculate it separately for the centre and edges, using each local PPFD and the same operating hours. A shelf average will not tell you what reached one pot in a dim corner.

Sometimes moving a single pot out of its neighbour’s shade gives it a better share of the light. If you adjust the fixture height, revisit the same points: the light will spread differently across the shelf. You can then see what reached the plant in the middle and those beside it.

## Window light keeps changing

A sunny midday reading can be especially misleading. Light may be abundant at that moment, even though the morning was overcast and a neighbouring building will soon block the sun. Multiplying that one reading by the whole day does not give the daily total.

To obtain the total, changing PPFD is recorded at regular intervals and accumulated with the duration of each interval. A logging meter makes this easier. Three scattered readings in the morning, afternoon and evening can help you explore a window, but do not replace a full daily series.

A lamp's contribution is easier to assess separately when daylight no longer interferes, provided its brightness remains constant. Daylight is another part of the daily amount. A lamp-only calculation cannot describe the total DLI for a plant beside a window.

## What is worth keeping

For your next comparison, keep the reading and units, the position at the leaves, the meter and settings, the fixture height and operating hours. For window light, add the date, time and cloud cover. Label a calculated DLI as either the lamp's contribution or the result of a daily recording.

Over time, these notes help you notice what is easy to miss in daily care: a plant has grown closer to the lamp, neighbouring leaves have shaded a smaller pot, or the autumn window has become dimmer. Each change gives you a reason to check, and each new reading has something to compare with.

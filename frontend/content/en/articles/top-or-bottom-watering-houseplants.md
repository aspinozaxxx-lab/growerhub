On one pot, a watering can's spout reaches the mix easily. On another, leaves cover the entire surface. Should you pour from above or fill a tray below?

**Both methods can work for an ordinary pot with drainage holes. Choose the one that lets you wet the root ball evenly and drain the excess. Bottom watering helps you work around dense foliage and keep the plant's base dry. Top watering reaches the mix directly and, when needed, can flush out accumulated salts.**

## First, decide whether the plant needs water

Watering direction does not change a plant's needs. Cyclamen and echeveria need different drying conditions, even when they share a windowsill. Check the mix and judge the pot's weight against the usual condition of that particular plant. A dry crust on the surface does not mean the whole root ball is dry.

If leaves droop while the pot is heavy and the mix remains wet, adding water will not solve the choice of method. First check that drainage is clear and the inner pot is not standing in water inside its decorative outer pot.

Both methods need holes. Remove the inner pot from an outer container without drainage and water it separately. A layer of stones at the bottom does not provide an outlet for excess water.

## Top watering: put the water on the mix

Set the pot somewhere you can collect and discard the runoff. Place a narrow spout beneath the leaves and water slowly across the accessible surface. Avoid pouring into the centre of a rosette or onto a sensitive plant base.

The aim is to wet the root ball, rather than leave one wet patch at the edge. Once water has passed through the mix and emerged from the holes, let the pot drain and empty the saucer. Do not return it to a puddle inside the outer pot.

Sometimes water rushes down the sides while the pot barely gains weight. Very dry mix can resist wetting, so fast runoff does not prove that water reached the roots. Apply water more slowly in small passes, allowing it to soak in, then check the root ball again. Avoid repeatedly filling the tray without checking the result.

## Bottom watering: let the mix draw water up

This can be convenient when dense foliage blocks access or you want to keep leaves and the plant's base dry. For a particular example, see the watering advice in our [guide to indoor cyclamen](/en/articles/indoor-cyclamen-cool-spot-and-watering/).

Put room temperature water in a clean, shallow container. Stand the inner pot in it so water reaches the drainage holes without flooding the top of the mix or the plant's base. The mix will draw moisture upwards.

Check whether the pot becomes noticeably heavier and moisture moves up through the root ball. When the surface starts to moisten and the root ball has absorbed water, lift the pot out, let it drain and discard what remains. Coarse bark, pot size and the mix affect how quickly this happens. One timer setting will not suit a whole collection; do not leave an ordinary pot soaking overnight.

## Choose for the pot in front of you

| What you see | Where to start | What to check afterwards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The mix is accessible beneath the leaves | Water slowly from above | The root ball is wet and excess water has drained |
| Dense foliage covers the surface | Try watering from below | Moisture has moved up and the pot is out of the water |
| A hard salt crust has appeared | Identify the deposit; flush from above with free drainage when appropriate | Runoff is discarded and the cause of salt buildup is addressed |
| Leaves wilt but the pot is heavy and wet | Hold off watering and check roots and drainage | No standing water remains in the outer pot |

## What switching methods cannot fix

Bottom watering does not flush dissolved salts out of the mix. If salts accumulate, the plant may need flushing from above with free drainage, along with a review of fertilizer or water. A pale patch can have different causes: our [guide to white crust or mold on houseplant soil](/en/articles/white-crust-or-mold-on-houseplant-soil/) explains how to distinguish hard deposits from fuzzy growth.

Pots designed with a reservoir and wick are a separate system, suited to particular plants and mixes. An ordinary pot left permanently in a full saucer does not become one.

At the next watering, choose one pot. Check it before watering, wet the root ball by the more convenient method and compare its weight after drainage. That gives you a useful reference for future checks. You can note the date, but use the plant and mix to decide when to water again.
